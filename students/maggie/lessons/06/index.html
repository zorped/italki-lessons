Fix it

Your speaking checks

These examples are lightly reconstructed from today's conversation. Say your correction first, then open the answer.

  • I think I should more practice.

    Say it naturally

    I think I should practise more.

    Should needs a base verb. Also possible: 'I think I need more practice.' Here, practice is a noun.

  • Yesterday I want to ask them…

    Say it naturally

    Yesterday I wanted to ask them…

    Notice yesterday first, then move the verb into the past. This is the same pattern we practised in your customer-meeting story.

  • Could you want to try more?

    Say it naturally

    Would you like some more?

    Would you like…? offers something. Could you try…? asks someone to do something. Want does not fit after could you in this offer.

  • Could you check whether is available?

    Say it naturally

    Could you check whether you are available?

    Include the subject. For a room or a time slot, say 'whether it is available'. The subject depends on what you mean.

  • Whether you are available on 9 a.m.

    Say it naturally

    whether you are available at 9 a.m.

    At + clock time; on + day; in + month or part of the day.

  • I will aware of this.

    Say it naturally

    I will be aware of this. / I will keep this in mind.

    Aware is an adjective. After will, use a verb: will be aware. Keep this in mind is a natural way to express your intention.

Compare · quiz

A bit, a little, some more

Several of your examples were already correct. Keep them, and notice what follows each phrase.

Before an adjective
a bit cold · a little cold · a little bit coldAll three mean slightly cold. A bit is conversational. No of here.
Before a noun
a little time · a bit of time · a little bit of timeUse of after a bit / a little bit before a noun. A little time does not need of.
With a comparison
a bit better · a little better · a little bit betterDo you feel a little better? All three patterns work.

Offer food naturally

Would you like some more soup?

Would you like a little more?

Would you like another piece of chicken?

Some more leaves the amount open. A little more suggests a small amount. Both are natural offers.

A clarification from our discussion: “Would you like a little bit more?” is also correct. “Could you try a little more?” can work when you are encouraging someone to taste or try something; it is a request rather than a neutral offer.

  1. I'm feeling     cold. Could we close the window?

  2. We need     time to finish this.

  3. Would you like some more    ?

  4. Would you like two more     of chicken?

Builder · quiz

If or whether?

First decide whether you are giving a condition or asking an indirect question.

A condition: if

We'll have the meeting if everyone is available.

The meeting depends on that condition.

An indirect question: if or whether

Could you check if you are available?

Could you check whether you are available?

Both ask the same yes/no question. The distinction is not the number of possible answers.

  1. Could you checkthe request
  2. whether you are availablesubject + verb
  3. at 9 a.m. on Monday?time + day

Keep the subjectWhether you are available · whether it is ready · whether there are any mistakes. Whether does not replace the subject. You can add or not, but you do not always need it.

  1. Could you check whether     available at 9 a.m.?

  2. We will start     everyone is ready.

  3. I wanted to ask     you have time today.

  4. We need to decide     to meet online or in person.

Compare · quiz

Will, would, going to

Choose the meaning you need. Would can be polite, but politeness is only one of its uses.

I will…
a decision now, a promise, willingness or a prediction“Can you help?” “Yes, I'll check it now.”
I am going to…
an existing intention; also a prediction based on present evidenceI've planned my evening. I'm going to review the lesson notes.
I would…
an imagined result, a polite expression, or a future viewed from the pastI would help if I had time. / Would you like some soup? / I thought I would have time.

These are useful starting patterns, not rules that make will and going to interchangeable in every sentence. For a regular habit, use the present simple: “When I have time, I review my notes.”

One flat tyre, three viewpoints

  1. Monday morning · deciding

    Sam finds that his bike has a flat tyre. “Can you fix it, Dad?” His dad decides to help: “Yes, I'll fix it tonight.”

  2. Monday afternoon · already planned

    Sam's mum asks about the bike. Dad says, “I'm going to fix it tonight. I've already promised Sam.”

  3. Tuesday · looking back

    Sam tells a friend, “Dad said he would fix it on Monday evening, and he did. I can ride it again.”

What changed?The repair is the same. I'll shows the decision as Dad makes it. I'm going to presents it as an existing plan. He would reports that future promise from a past viewpoint. Would alone does not tell us whether he actually repaired it; “and he did” supplies that information.

  1. The phone is ringing.     answer it.

  2. I've already decided.     review the notes tonight.

  3. Yesterday I thought I     have time to review, but I was too busy.

  4. If I had less work, I     help with the report.

Three ways to talk about mistakes

I make mistakes. A general fact or habit.

I am making mistakes. What is happening now or in the current period.

I have been making mistakes. A repeated or continuing pattern leading up to now.

After whether, the same forms work: “I don't know whether I'm making mistakes.”

Review

Useful pairs to keep

between · among
between
distinct things, choices or relationshipsWhat is the difference between a bit and a little?
among
within a group or surrounded by its membersWe shared the ideas among our colleagues.

It is not simply two versus three or more. Between can connect more than two distinct participants: negotiations between three companies.

brief · briefing · briefly
brief
verb: give essential information; adjective: shortCould you brief me on the changes? / a brief call
briefing
noun: an information sessionWe had a briefing before the workshop.
briefly
adverb: in a short way or for a short timePlease explain it briefly.
recurring · ongoing
recurring
happening repeatedlyThis issue recurs every Friday.
ongoing
continuingThe investigation is ongoing.

A problem can recur daily too. The size of the break does not determine whether recurring is possible.

in · on · at · next
at
clock timeat 9 a.m.
on
day or dateon Friday
in
month or part of the dayin October · in the afternoon
next / this
no preposition in these time expressionsSee you next lesson. / See you this Friday.

“See you Friday” is also natural informal English. “Next Friday” can be understood differently, so use a date when scheduling needs precision.
