1

Rule · fix · quiz

Much or many? Little or few?

First ask: can I count it? One task, two tasks: yes. One information, two informations: no.

Can count

tasks · meetings · delays

  • many tasks
  • a few tasks
  • fewer meetings

Can't count

work · time · information

  • much work
  • a little time
  • less time
a lot of works with both: a lot of tasks, a lot of work.

Your sentences

Correct each one out loud, then open it.

  • I work too much tasks at the same time.

    Say it naturally

    I work on too many tasks at the same time.

    Tasks can be counted, so: many.

  • I planned too much work and activities for today.

    Say it naturally

    I planned too much work and too many activities for today.

    Work is uncountable; activities are countable. Each noun needs its own word.

  • I spent a few time taking care of my child.

    Say it naturally

    I spent a little time taking care of my child.

    Time (meaning hours and minutes) is uncountable: a little time.

  • Every week I took three class with a teacher.

    Say it naturally

    Every week I took three classes with a teacher.

    After a number, a countable noun needs -s. Say it clearly: class-es.

One thing needs a

If you can count it and there is only one, put a (or the) in front.

  • Obsessive effort on English learning has trade-off.

    Say it naturally

    It has a trade-off.

    One countable thing needs a in front of it: a trade-off, a breakthrough, a setback.

  • We just have, at least, bachelor degree.

    Say it naturally

    We just need at least a bachelor's degree.

    Degree is countable, so it needs a. Note the spelling: bachelor's degree.

  3. This month we had     delays than last month.

2

Fix it

Words that go together

Some verbs need -ing after them, some need a small word like for or into, and some need none. Learn them as one piece.

  • I didn't continue to learning.

    Say it naturally

    I didn't continue learning.

    continue + -ing. (Continue to learn is also correct, but never to + -ing.)

  • It consumes much time for him to finish the work.

    Say it naturally

    It takes him a lot of time to finish the work.

    For time, use take. Consume sounds like fuel or energy being used up.

  • I crave to see my idol this year.

    Say it naturally

    I'm eager to see my idol this year.

    Crave takes a noun: I crave the chance to see my idol. For a verb, use be eager to or long to.

  • My listening will have a breakthrough.

    Say it naturally

    I will make a breakthrough in my listening.

    A person makes a breakthrough in something.

  • I usually spend myself too thin.

    Say it naturally

    I usually spread myself too thin.

    A fixed expression: to try to do too many things at once.

  • I always confuse this word with destiny.

    Say it naturally

    I always mix up fate and destiny.

    Mix up A and B is the everyday way to say you confuse two things.

  • I spend massive amounts of time to climb the mountain.

    Say it naturally

    I spent a lot of time climbing the mountain.

    spend time + -ing: spend time reading, spend time climbing. Never spend time to.

  • If we want to apply our company's job…

    Say it naturally

    If you want to apply for a job at our company…

    We apply for a job, a visa, a course.

  • Then you message to me.

    Say it naturally

    Then you message me.

    Message, email, call and tell take the person directly, with no to.

  • So I repeat: the obsessive learning English has trade-off.

    Say it naturally

    Learning English obsessively has a trade-off.

    An adverb (-ly) describes an action. An adjective describes a thing: obsessive effort, but learn obsessively.

Model

Telling a story about the past

When you described how you started learning English, you moved between present and past. Put a time phrase at the start, then keep every verb in the past.

  1. Five years ago

    I enrolled in several English classes.

  2. After a few months

    I realised I had made a breakthrough in my English listening.

  3. Later

    I didn't continue learning regularly.

Had made shows something happened before another past moment: the breakthrough came first, then you realised it.

4

Fix it · quiz

Will or present?

When you talked about your free time, you often used will for things you usually do. In English, habits use the present simple.

Your sentences

  • If I have time, I will book many exhibitions or activities.

    Say it naturally

    When I have time, I usually book exhibitions or other activities.

    For something you often do, use the present: I usually book. Will is for plans and promises.

  • When I face setbacks, I will talk with my friends.

    Say it naturally

    When I face setbacks, I talk to my friends about how to solve them.

    This is what you normally do, so use the present simple.

Present simple

habits, routines, things that are usually true

  • I usually go hiking.
  • I talk to my friends.

will

promises, decisions now, predictions

  • I will send it today.
  • I think I'll go.

Check yourself

  1. On Sundays I usually     late.

  2. Don't worry, I     the report tonight.

  3. When I'm tired after work, I     a walk.

  4. It's raining. I think I     the bus today.

For reference. Read the example, not just the meaning.

fate · destiny · luck · chance
fate
what will happen, and you can't change itHe accepted his fate.
destiny
what you are meant to do or becomeShe felt teaching was her destiny.
luck
good or bad things that happen by chanceGood luck helped, but she also prepared.
chance
something random, or an opportunityI met him by chance.

Fate and destiny suggest a fixed future. Luck and chance don't.

area · field · realm
area
the everyday word for a topicListening is one area I want to improve.
field
a subject of work or studyLogistics is her field.
realm
formal; mostly in writingThat is outside my realm of expertise.

When unsure, use area.

genuine · true · genuinely · truly
genuine
real, sincere (adjective)Her interest was genuine.
true
correct, a fact (adjective)The story is true.
genuinely
really, sincerely (adverb)I was genuinely surprised.
truly
really (adverb)It was a truly good result.

Adjectives describe nouns; adverbs describe verbs and adjectives.

long for · crave · want · be eager to
long for
+ noun; want something for a long timeI long for a change.
crave
+ noun; a strong physical or emotional desireI crave some quiet time.
want
neutral, always safeI want to improve my listening.
be eager to
+ verb; excited to do somethingI'm eager to meet my idol.

Long for + noun, long to + verb. Crave never takes to + verb.

Speaking

Say it out loud

Speak for 30–60 seconds on each. Record yourself if you can, then listen for much/many and past tense.

  1. 1

    Describe a busy week at work. Use many, much, a little and fewer.

    Compare with an example answer

    Last week I had too many meetings and too much paperwork, so I had only a little time to plan. Next week I want fewer meetings.

  2. 2

    Tell your English-learning story again. Start with Five years ago and keep it in the past.

    Compare with an example answer

    Five years ago, I enrolled in an English class. After a few months, I realised I had made a breakthrough in my listening. Later, I got busy and didn't continue learning regularly.

  3. 3

    Describe a problem at work. Was it a setback, an obstacle, a delay or a failure? Why?

    Compare with an example answer

    Last year a key supplier closed. It was a setback, not a failure: the main obstacle was finding a new one quickly, and it caused a two-week delay.

  4. 4

    What are you eager to do this year? Do you ever spread yourself too thin?

    Compare with an example answer

    I tend to spread myself too thin. I'm eager to improve my English, but I have too many other projects.
